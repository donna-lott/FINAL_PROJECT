The Georgian Writing System: History, Evolution, and Heritage

Overview and Unique Cultural Status

Georgian Language has its own unique writing system. Unlike widespread scripts such as Latin, which are shared across multiple nations, the Georgian alphabet is used primarily by the Georgian people. A defining structural feature of the script is its phonemic precision: every letter corresponds to a specific, single sound. The three scripts are written horizontally from left to right.

In 2016, UNESCO inscribed the "Living culture of three writing systems of the Georgian alphabet" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. This designation recognized Georgia's unique global distinction of maintaining three living historical scripts that remain active within its culture.

The Three Historical Scripts and Modern Usage

Throughout its historical evolution, the Georgian writing system developed through three distinct stages, each possessing its own graphic style:

  • Asomtavruli (Mrglovani): Meaning "capital" or "rounded" letters, Asomtavruli is the oldest known form of the alphabet. Characterized by monumental, linear, and rounded geometric shapes, it is attested in 5th-century inscriptions such as those at Bolnisi Sioni and Bir el Qutt. It is a unicameral script, meaning it does not distinguish between upper and lower cases. Although later replaced for continuous text, it was retained for epigraphs, headings, and illuminated capital letters.
  • Nuskhuri: Emerging in the 9th century (with early specimens such as the Ateni Sioni inscription of 835 AD), Nuskhuri, or "book letters," evolved as a simplified, cursive adaptation of Asomtavruli. Its rounded and fluid angularity enabled scribes to copy ecclesiastical manuscripts and religious texts more efficiently.
  • Mkhedruli: Mkhedruli first appearing in the late 10th century (982–986 AD) and becoming prominent in the 11th–12th centuries. It originated as a streamlined writing system for civil administration, royal charters, and secular literature.

By the 12th century, all three scripts coexisted. Asomtavruli and Nuskhuri were utilized primarily for liturgical and religious texts, whereas Mkhedruli was employed for secular matters. Today, Mkhedruli serves as the standard script for all aspects of modern life, including education, media, literature, and digital communication. Asomtavruli and Nuskhuri remain in active ceremonial use by the Georgian Orthodox Church for religious texts and iconography.

Historical Overview and Orthographic Evolution

The precise origins of the Georgian script remain a topic of scholarly discussion:

  • Pre-Christian Hypotheses: The 11th-century historian Leonti Mroveli, writing in the Lives of the Kings of Kartli, attributes the creation of Georgian literacy to King Pharnavaz I of Iberia in the 3rd century BC. Modern academic consensus views this narrative as legendary due to a lack of contemporary archaeological evidence, or interprets it as the historical introduction of alloglottography—using foreign scripts (such as Aramaic or Greek) to record the Georgian language.
  • Christianization Period: Most scholars connect the creation of the written script to the adoption of Christianity in Iberia (c. 326 or 337 AD), which necessitated the translation of Biblical and liturgical works. The earliest confirmed archaeological examples of Asomtavruli date to the 5th century AD.
  • The Mashtots Controversy: Medieval Armenian accounts by Koryun attribute the creation of the Georgian script to the Armenian scholar Mesrop Mashtots around 405–408 AD. Georgian scholars, including Ivane Javakhishvili and Tamaz Gamkrelidze, refute this claim, noting that the passage in Koryun is a later 6th–7th century interpolation, that Mashtots did not know the Georgian language, and that the structural and graphic design of Georgian differs fundamentally from Armenian.
  • Structural Influence and Reforms: Structurally, the Georgian alphabet mirrors the order and numerical system of the Greek alphabet, with unique Georgian consonant sounds appended at the end. Originally composed of 38 letters, the alphabet underwent a major modernization reform in the 1860s led by Ilia Chavchavadze and his contemporaries. This reform eliminated five obsolete letters no longer used in spoken Georgian, establishing the modern 33-letter alphabet consisting of 5 vowels and 28 consonants.

Georgian Script

The Three Scripts of the Georgian Language The Georgian language is written using three unique scripts: Asomtavruli, Nuskhuri, and Mkhedruli. Each of these scripts has its own history, characteristics, and uses, reflecting the evolution of Georgian writing over the centuries. Asomtavruli is the oldest of the three scripts, with the earliest known inscriptions dating back to the 5th century. The name Asomtavruli translates to "capital letters," derived from the Georgian words aso (letter) and mtavari (principal or head). This script is also known as Mrgvlovani, meaning "rounded," due to its distinctive round letter shapes. Asomtavruli was primarily used for inscriptions and religious texts, particularly in the early Christian period of Georgia. Although it was gradually replaced by Nuskhuri in religious manuscripts, Asomtavruli continued to be used for titles and decorative purposes in later centuries. Nuskhuri, the second Georgian script, emerged around the 9th century as a graphic variant of Asomtavruli. The name Nuskhuri comes from the Georgian word nuskha, meaning "inventory" or "schedule." This script was primarily used in religious manuscripts, often in combination with Asomtavruli for illuminated capitals. The combination of Nuskhuri and Asomtavruli is known as Khutsuri, which translates to "clerical." Khutsuri was widely used in hagiography and other religious texts until the 19th century. Mkhedruli, the third and most recent script, became the dominant writing system for the Georgian language. The name Mkhedruli means "cavalry" or "military," derived from the word mkhedari, meaning "horseman" or "warrior." Mkhedruli first appeared in the 10th century and was initially used for royal charters, historical documents, and non-religious manuscripts. Over time, it became the standard script for writing Georgian, replacing Khutsuri in most secular contexts. Mkhedruli is a unicameral script, meaning it does not distinguish between uppercase and lowercase letters. However, a variant known as Mkhedruli Mtavruli, or simply Mtavruli, was developed for capital letters, though its use is now largely decorative. The three scripts share the same alphabetical order and letter names, but they differ in appearance and historical usage. Asomtavruli and Nuskhuri are primarily associated with the Georgian Orthodox Church, where they are still used in ceremonial religious texts and iconography. Mkhedruli, on the other hand, is the script used for modern Georgian and its related Kartvelian languages, such as Mingrelian, Laz, and Svan. The scripts are a testament to the rich linguistic and cultural heritage of Georgia, recognized both nationally and internationally for their historical and cultural significance.
